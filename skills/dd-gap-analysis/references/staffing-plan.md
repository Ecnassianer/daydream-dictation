# Gap Analysis: Staffing Plans

Read this file only during a `dd-gap-analysis` run, and only when the document argues for adding people to a team: a headcount request, a hiring plan, or a pitch for more staff.

## What a good staffing plan has

- **The problem in business terms.** What the team can't do today, and what that costs: missed dates, lost revenue, risk, burnout.
- **Current capacity against demand**, with numbers where possible: who is on the team, what they're on, and the work that doesn't fit.
- **The specific roles**, with level, responsibilities, and why each one closes part of the gap.
- **The cost**: salary bands, recruiting, equipment, and management overhead.
- **A timeline**: when each hire starts, how long ramp-up takes, and when the benefit shows up.
- **Alternatives considered**: reprioritizing, contractors, tooling, borrowing from another team, and why they fall short.
- **What happens if the answer is no**, and what a partial yes would buy.
- **How success will be measured** after the hires land.

## Questions

Ask these after the core *reader needs* question, in this order.

### Who decides, and what do they need to hear?

Name the decision-maker the plan is written for, as far as the document says, and what they care about: budget, deadlines, risk, strategy. Flag a plan pitched at the wrong concerns, such as team morale for a reader who decides on revenue.

### Is the gap quantified?

Check for numbers on current capacity and demand: headcount, allocation, backlog, missed or at-risk dates. Flag claims like "we're stretched thin" that have nothing to back them.

### Is each role justified?

For every role requested, check that the document says its level and responsibilities and which part of the gap it closes. Flag roles that are listed but not argued for, and parts of the gap no role covers.

### Are cost and timeline stated?

Check for a cost per role or in total, start dates, ramp-up time, and when the benefit arrives. Flag a plan that asks for headcount without saying what it costs or when it pays off.

### Were alternatives considered?

Check whether the plan addresses reprioritizing, contractors, tooling, or borrowing staff, and says why each falls short. A decision-maker will ask; the plan should answer first.

### What if the answer is no, or partly no?

Check whether the plan says what gets cut or slips without the hires, and which hire matters most if only some are approved. Flag a plan that is all-or-nothing by omission.

### How will success be measured?

Check for a way to tell, six or twelve months later, that the hires worked. Skip this question if the decision-maker has said they don't want one.
